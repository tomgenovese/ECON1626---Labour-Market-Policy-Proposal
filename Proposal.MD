# AI and the Australian Labour Market: A Policy Proposal
 
**Prepared for:** Deputy Secretary, Employment and Workplace Relations Policy, Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR) — Minister: Amanda Rishworth

**Responds to:** *AI and Employment in Australia* (DEWR Office of the Chief Economist, 8 July 2026) and the ongoing labour-market monitoring framework it establishes

**Author:** Thomas Genovese s4002577
 
---
 
This brief is addressed to the Deputy Secretary, Employment and Workplace Relations Policy, DEWR, and responds directly to *AI and Employment in Australia* (Office of the Chief Economist, 8 July 2026) and the quarterly exposure-based monitoring framework the report commits the department to running.
 
## Executive Summary
 
AI is not yet destroying Australian jobs — it is quietly narrowing who gets hired into them. Between November 2022 and February 2026, employment in the most AI-exposed occupations (clerical, administrative, data-entry work) grew 5.6%, against 9.5% in the least-exposed occupations: a 3.9-percentage-point gap in the same economy over the same period, running roughly 2% below the pre-generative-AI trend. Because this operates through suppressed hiring rather than layoffs, headline unemployment does not detect it — DEWR's own monitoring framework exists precisely to close that blind spot. This brief recommends the department move from monitoring the gap to acting on it: a costed, automatically-triggered hiring subsidy for junior and graduate roles in affected industries, activated once the exposure gap DEWR already measures crosses an agreed threshold, benchmarked against the cost structure of the 2020 JobMaker Hiring Credit.
 
## Problem Definition & Context
 
Three channels are at work, each grounded in the July 2026 report's own findings. **Task automation/augmentation:** AI performs routine cognitive tasks — data entry, scheduling, first-pass drafting, basic client correspondence — that make up the bulk of clerical and administrative work (ANZSCO Major Group 5). **Industrial restructuring:** the effect is task-specific, not economy-wide — software development employment grew 25% over the same period the report tracks, showing AI is reshaping which occupations grow, not shrinking labour demand overall. **Wages and job design:** this is the weakest evidence base of the three, and the brief is deliberately specific rather than trying to paper over that. The most directly relevant OECD study (Georgieff & Broecke 2024) found no association between an occupation's AI exposure and its overall wage growth, and — notably — found AI exposure associated with narrower within-occupation pay inequality in a small set of professional occupations. But that study covers 2014–18, entirely before generative AI's mainstream adoption from late 2022, so it says little about the current episode DEWR is now tracking. That gap in the wage evidence is itself a reason for DEWR's monitoring framework to start collecting occupation-level wage data alongside the employment-growth data it already reports.
 
The mechanism that ties this together: when a job is one AI performs well, employers are not sacking incumbent staff — they are simply not backfilling departures and posting fewer new roles. The job count looks normal because unemployment measures people who lost jobs and are searching, not jobs that were never created. That is precisely the blind spot DEWR's monitoring framework was built to see past.
 
## Analysis of Impacts
 
**Employment:** 5.6% growth in AI-exposed occupations vs 9.5% in the least-exposed, a 3.9-point gap; high-exposure roles running ~2% below their pre-ChatGPT trend.
 
**Equity/distribution:** High-exposure roles are 43.7% male (so ~56% female) against 69.5% male in low-exposure roles — the effect concentrates on women. High-exposure occupations are 43.7% bachelor's-degree-or-higher against 14.9% in low-exposure occupations — the effect concentrates on graduates, because entry-level office roles are exactly where AI performs well, narrowing the first rung of the career ladder specifically for the cohort with the least existing labour-market attachment to fall back on.
 
**Tasks and industrial structure:** the 25% growth in software development against 5.6% in clerical/admin work is direct evidence this is task-specific restructuring, not aggregate job destruction — a distinction that matters for which policy lever is appropriate (see below).
 
**Important nuance, stated directly rather than glossed over:** DEWR's own public framing of the July 2026 report is that there is "no evidence of broad disruption" and "occupational reshuffling has not accelerated." This brief does not dispute that. The 3.9-point gap is an early, moderate signal, not a crisis. That is the argument *for* acting now, while intervention is still cheap and precautionary, rather than waiting for a clearer signal that would only arrive after the effect is harder to reverse and more expensive to fix.
 
## Policy Options
 
**Option 1 — Pre-emptive (in-work) retraining eligibility.** Currently, subsidised retraining generally requires unemployment first. Flip this: workers in occupations DEWR's framework classifies as high-exposure become eligible for free TAFE/short-course retraining while still employed. *Mechanism:* eligibility rule change, no new payment infrastructure. *Targets:* incumbent workers in ANZSCO Major Group 5 roles. *Cost:* moderate — scales with uptake, not headcount, since it is opt-in. *Honest limitation:* the adult-retraining efficacy evidence base is genuinely mixed; this option's value is precautionary, not proven.
 
**Option 2 — Automatic monitoring-triggered support.** DEWR already collects the exposure-gap data quarterly; currently it is only published in reports. Instead: pre-agree that if the AI-exposed vs non-exposed employment growth gap widens beyond the 3.9-point baseline the July 2026 report established — say, by a further 2 percentage points in a rolling four-quarter window — a support package activates automatically, without a fresh ministerial decision each time. *Cost:* near-zero; the data pipeline already exists. *Limitation:* a trigger alone delivers nothing — it only tells the department when to act.
 
**Option 3 — Entry-level hiring subsidy tied to training.** A per-position subsidy for employers hiring junior/graduate staff in affected industries, conditional on a genuine, auditable training component. *Costing benchmark:* the 2020 JobMaker Hiring Credit paid $200/week for under-30 hires for up to 12 months (~$10,400 per position at full duration), at a total program cost of roughly $4 billion for approximately 450,000 positions nationally (~$8,900 average realised cost per position, since not every position ran the full 12 months). A comparably-scaled but narrower scheme — targeted only at high-exposure clerical/admin industries rather than economy-wide — would cost meaningfully less in aggregate while directly addressing the graduate effect identified above. *Limitation:* real deadweight-cost risk, since some subsidised hires would have occurred anyway.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Pre-emptive retraining | Moderate — mixed evidence on adult retraining | Good — broad coverage | High — extends existing scheme | Moderate, opt-in |
| 2. Automatic trigger | High — reuses existing data | N/A alone — a mechanism, not a benefit | High — administrative only | Near-zero |
| 3. Hiring subsidy | Moderate — deadweight-cost risk | High — targets graduates directly | Moderate — needs employer take-up | High but boundable (JobMaker-scale benchmark) |
 
## Recommended Package & Implementation Plan
 
Combine Options 2 and 3: the automatic trigger (Option 2) activates the hiring subsidy (Option 3) once the exposure gap crosses the pre-agreed threshold, rather than running the subsidy as a permanent standing commitment or requiring fresh ministerial sign-off each time the data moves.
 
- **Timeline:** threshold-setting and legislative/administrative design in Q1–Q2 2027 (six months), timed to land before DEWR's next scheduled monitoring update following the classification transition noted in the July 2026 report; pilot activation-ready from Q3 2027.
- **Responsibilities:** DEWR owns the monitoring framework and the trigger mechanism; Jobs and Skills Australia administers subsidy payments and verifies the training condition, mirroring the JobMaker administrative split between Treasury and the ATO.
- **Costs:** capped and scaled to trigger severity — e.g. a subsidy rate benchmarked at $150–200/week per position, for up to 12 months, restricted to ANZSCO Major Group 5 industries rather than economy-wide, which bounds total exposure well below JobMaker's $4bn economy-wide cost.
- **KPIs:** narrow the 3.9-point exposure gap by a specified margin within four quarters of activation; track graduate employment share in high-exposure industries against the current 43.7% baseline; report both against DEWR's existing quarterly monitoring cycle rather than a new reporting instrument.
## Risks & Mitigations
 
A threshold set too high means the policy never activates when needed; too low erodes credibility through over-triggering. *Mitigation:* independent review of the threshold tied to DEWR's own monitoring cadence. Deadweight cost in the subsidy is real. *Mitigation:* the auditable training condition and a hard sunset clause, not an open-ended commitment. Retraining that fails to translate into re-employment is a known risk in the literature. *Mitigation:* treat Option 1 as precautionary and low-cost rather than the primary lever, consistent with its honestly-stated mixed evidence base above.
