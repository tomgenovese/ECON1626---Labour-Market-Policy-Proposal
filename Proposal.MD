# Securing the Signal: Protecting Australia's AI Employment Monitoring Series and Giving It Consequence
 
**Prepared for:** Deputy Secretary, Employment and Workplace Relations Policy, Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR)
 
**Portfolio Minister:** the Hon Amanda Rishworth MP, Minister for Employment and Workplace Relations
 
**Responds to:** *AI and employment in Australia: Monitoring framework and evidence to date* (DEWR Office of the Chief Economist, 8 July 2026), and the quarterly monitoring cadence committed to at p. 14 of that report
 
**Author:** Thomas Genovese (s4002577)
 
---
 
This brief recommends that DEWR protect its planned quarterly AI-exposure monitoring series through the ABS occupational classification transition, and attach to it a pre-agreed threshold that activates a capped, pre-legislated hiring subsidy for junior roles in high-exposure occupations.
 
## Executive Summary
 
AI is not yet destroying Australian jobs, but it is slowing hiring into the occupations it touches. Between November 2022 and February 2026, employment in the most AI-exposed fifth of occupations grew 5.6 per cent against 9.5 per cent in the least-exposed fifth — a result DEWR reads as "justification for ongoing monitoring rather than clear evidence that AI has reduced employment" (DEWR 2026a). This brief agrees, and addresses two gaps in what follows. First, the series DEWR plans to run quarterly cannot resume until late 2026, because the ABS is ceasing to publish employment by ANZSCO occupation and the framework must be rebuilt on OSCA. Second, monitoring carries no consequence: there is no pre-agreed point at which a widening gap does anything. Securing the series and rule-binding it costs almost nothing, and converts an analytical finding into a standing instrument.
 
## Problem Definition and Context
 
Australia's AI transition is early: 12 per cent of businesses used AI in 2024–25, concentrated in information and telecommunications, professional services and finance (ABS 2026a). Jobs and Skills Australia's task-level analysis, which supplies the exposure scores DEWR's model uses, found only 4 per cent of the workforce in occupations with high automation exposure against 79 per cent with low exposure (JSA 2025).
 
Low adoption with concentrated exposure explains the shape of the signal. Employers facing partial automation rarely dismiss incumbents; they slow backfilling and post fewer roles. Headline unemployment counts people who lost jobs and are searching, not roles never created, so adjustment on the hiring margin escapes the indicators watched most closely. DEWR finds the hiring rate at a relatively low level while noting strong alternative explanations (DEWR 2026a) — which is why a continuous series, not one observation, is needed to tell them apart.
 
That series is now at risk. DEWR plans ongoing quarterly monitoring on this framework, but states "updates will not be possible until late 2026" because the ABS Labour Force Survey transition ceases publishing employment by ANZSCO occupation, requiring conversion to the Occupation Standard Classification for Australia (DEWR 2026a, p. 14; ABS 2024, 2026b). The exposure scores are ANZSCO-based, so the measurement chain must be rebuilt across a structural break — precisely when continuity matters most.
 
## Analysis of Impacts
 
**Employment and industry.** The 3.9 percentage point cumulative gap over 39 months equates to an annualised differential of roughly 1.1 percentage points (author's calculation from DEWR 2026a). DEWR's core model (355 occupations, quarterly from February 2015) estimates about 2 per cent lower employment for an occupation one standard deviation above average exposure — but loses significance under two alternative exposure measures and when COVID-19 is excluded from the pre-treatment sample, while the job-advertisements model shows no negative relationship at all (DEWR 2026a). The finding is real and fragile at once. It is compositional rather than aggregate: software and applications programmers, themselves highly exposed, reached 199,000 by February 2026, 25 per cent above November 2022. AI is redistributing labour demand, not reducing it.
 
**Wages and job design.** This is the weakest link, and DEWR says so: the report expressly does not measure AI's relationship to wages, productivity or job quality, calling that "a potential future extension" (DEWR 2026a). The OECD found AI had not altered the gap between high- and low-wage occupations, but its data cover 2014–18, pre-generative AI (OECD 2024). The strongest recent Australian estimate, on linked administrative data, finds earnings and hiring outcomes in more-exposed occupations no worse overall — though younger exposed workers show signs of lower hiring rates (Gross 2026).
 
**Distribution and equity.** DEWR distinguishes two mechanisms, and the distinction drives the policy design. Women are exposed through *occupational composition*: men are 43.7 per cent of workers in the most-exposed occupations against 69.5 per cent in the least exposed (DEWR 2026a, Table 2) — corroborated by the ILO, which finds 29 per cent of employment in female-dominated occupations exposed to generative AI against 16 per cent in male-dominated ones (Butt and Berg 2026).
 
Young people are exposed *within* occupations: AI performs best at tasks requiring codified knowledge and is less capable of replacing tacit knowledge accumulated through experience, which younger workers disproportionately lack (DEWR 2026a). Composition data therefore do not settle the question — the most-exposed quintile is only 11.4 per cent aged 15–24, below the 15.3 per cent workforce average, and employment for 20–24 year olds has grown slightly faster than for those 25 and over. Exposed occupations are also degree-heavy (43.7 per cent bachelor's or higher against 14.9 per cent), but that too is composition. The entry-level case is therefore prospective, resting on task structure rather than a measured downturn.
 
## Policy Options
 
**Option 1 — In-work retraining eligibility.** Extend fee-free TAFE and short-course eligibility to workers already in high-exposure occupations, rather than only after job loss — an eligibility rule change needing no new payment infrastructure. *Scale:* opt-in, so cost tracks uptake. *Limitation:* adult retraining evidence is mixed; the value is precautionary.
 
**Option 2 — Secure and rule-bind the quarterly series.** Fund the OSCA conversion as a priority deliverable with a published back-cast so pre- and post-break estimates stay comparable, and pre-agree that support activates when the annualised growth differential exceeds 2 percentage points for two consecutive quarters — a clear escalation from the current 1.1. *Scale:* administrative; the method exists. *Limitation:* a trigger delivers nothing by itself.
 
**Option 3 — Capped entry-level hiring subsidy.** A per-position payment for employers hiring junior staff into high-exposure occupations, conditional on an auditable training component. *Scale:* $200 per week for up to 12 months ($10,400 per position, matching JobMaker's rate), capped at an illustrative 20,000 positions — maximum exposure near $208 million. *Limitation:* deadweight cost and demonstrated take-up risk.
 
| Option | Efficiency | Equity | Feasibility | Fiscal cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. In-work retraining | Moderate — mixed evidence | Good — broad coverage | High — extends existing schemes | Moderate |
| 2. Secure and rule-bind series | High — reuses existing method | Neutral — a mechanism, not a benefit | High — administrative | Negligible |
| 3. Entry-level subsidy | Moderate — deadweight risk | High — targets exposed entry points | Moderate — take-up dependent | Capped (~$208m) |
 
## Recommended Package and Implementation Plan
 
Adopt Options 2 and 3 as one instrument: the quarterly indicator activates the pre-legislated subsidy, removing the need for fresh ministerial decision each time the data moves. This matches the Productivity Commission's position that emerging technology be managed through monitoring and existing frameworks rather than pre-emptive intervention (PC 2025), while ensuring monitoring carries a consequence.
 
- **Timeline.** Q4 2026: OSCA conversion delivered with back-cast, series resumes. Q1 2027: threshold methodology published for consultation. Q2 2027: subsidy legislated in dormant form, activation-ready Q3 2027.
- **Responsibilities.** DEWR's Office of the Chief Economist owns the series and certifies breaches; Jobs and Skills Australia maintains exposure scores; the ATO administers payments through Single Touch Payroll — data DEWR has already flagged for incorporation into the framework.
- **Costs.** Series: within existing departmental resourcing. Subsidy: nil until activation, capped as above, with a hard 24-month sunset.
- **KPIs.** (1) Series resumes on the OSCA basis by Q4 2026 with a comparable back-cast. (2) Occupation-level earnings incorporated by Q4 2027, closing the gap DEWR identifies. (3) On activation, annualised differential returned below 2 points within four quarters. (4) Take-up above 50 per cent of the position cap — the metric JobMaker failed.
## Risks and Mitigations
 
**Take-up failure is the dominant risk.** JobMaker was budgeted at $4 billion for approximately 450,000 positions but supported around 5,278 at a revised cost near $93 million (Treasury 2020; Treasury FOI figures reported in ABC News 2021). *Mitigation:* a simple headcount eligibility test, burden pre-tested with employers, and take-up treated as a KPI, not an assumption.
 
**Series discontinuity.** The ANZSCO–OSCA break could render pre- and post-transition estimates incomparable, disabling the trigger. *Mitigation:* the back-cast, and a rule barring the trigger from firing on the first two post-transition quarters.
 
**Measurement risk.** Exposure scores estimate technical potential as at 2025, not realised adoption, and could date as capabilities change — a limitation DEWR acknowledges. *Mitigation:* pair the exposure gap with the ABS adoption series so activation requires both.
 
**Deadweight cost.** Some subsidised hires would occur anyway. *Mitigation:* the training condition, position cap and sunset clause bound fiscal exposure regardless.
