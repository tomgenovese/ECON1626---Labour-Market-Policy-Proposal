# Protecting Entry-Level Pathways in Australia's Most AI-Exposed Occupations

> **Prepared for:** Deputy Secretary, Employment and Workplace Relations Policy, Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR)  
> **Policy context:** This proposal responds to DEWR's *AI and employment in Australia: Monitoring framework and evidence to date* and recommends how its monitoring framework should identify and respond to deterioration in entry-level employment in occupations highly exposed to generative-AI automation (DEWR 2026a).

---

## Executive Summary

Australia is not currently experiencing broad AI-driven job loss. However, DEWR finds that employment growth has been weaker in occupations more exposed to AI automation: between November 2022 and February 2026, employment in the most exposed fifth grew 5.6%, compared with 9.5% in the least exposed fifth (DEWR 2026a). Jobs and Skills Australia (JSA) nevertheless finds no clear evidence yet that generative AI is reducing entry-level roles (JSA 2025a).

The policy problem is therefore not mass unemployment, but the risk that routine junior tasks are automated before clear job losses appear. DEWR should establish an AI Entry-Level Employment Monitor, pilot 5,000 AI-Augmented Entry Pathway places, and retain a targeted hiring credit if sustained deterioration emerges.

## Problem Definition and Context

DEWR's July 2026 report concludes that there is no evidence of broad AI-driven labour-market upheaval, but outcomes in highly exposed occupations warrant continued monitoring (DEWR 2026a). Its statistical model estimates employment around 2% below the pre-ChatGPT trend for an occupation one standard deviation above average AI exposure by February 2026. However, this relationship loses statistical significance under alternative exposure measures and when the COVID-19 period is removed, meaning it cannot establish causation (DEWR 2026a).

The narrower concern is entry-level work. JSA's task-level analysis finds that augmentation generally outweighs automation, but automation potential is more concentrated in routine roles (JSA 2025b). Junior workers commonly perform routine information-processing and drafting tasks while accumulating occupation-specific knowledge. Firms may retain experienced employees while reducing or redesigning new-entry positions.

JSA currently finds no clear evidence of declining entry-level roles due to generative AI (JSA 2025a). Economy-wide intervention is therefore premature. The policy objective should instead be to monitor entry pathways closely and preserve them where measurable deterioration emerges, without protecting obsolete tasks or discouraging productive AI adoption.

## Labour-Market Impacts

The likely first-order effect is task restructuring rather than whole-job replacement. JSA distinguishes automation, where generative AI can perform a task, from augmentation, where it assists a worker, and finds augmentation potential is generally larger across the labour market (JSA 2025b).

This creates a human-capital risk. Entry-level jobs help workers acquire firm-specific knowledge, judgement and client-facing skills. If routine learning tasks disappear, employers may demand more capable entrants while providing fewer opportunities to develop those capabilities.

The wage evidence remains limited. DEWR's current framework does not estimate AI's effects on wages, productivity or job quality (DEWR 2026a). Policy should therefore focus on observable hiring and employment outcomes rather than assume wage effects not yet established in Australian evidence.

## Policy Options

### Option 1 — AI Entry-Level Employment Monitor

**Mechanism.** DEWR's Office of the Chief Economist would add an entry-level module to its existing quarterly AI-employment framework. It would combine JSA exposure scores with ABS Labour Force Survey and JSA vacancy data to track younger and new-entry employment, vacancies and total occupational employment.

Occupations would enter an AI Entry-Risk Watchlist if they are in the highest quartile of JSA automation exposure and show weaker entry-level outcomes relative to their pre-generative-AI trend and less-exposed occupations for two consecutive quarters.

**Target.** Entry pathways into occupations with the highest automation exposure.

**Cost.** Approximately $0.95 million over three years. A dedicated team of one EL1 and one APS6 employee would cost $258,885 annually at DEWR's maximum 2026 salary rates: $143,099 for an EL1 and $115,786 for an APS6 (DEWR 2024). DEWR's enterprise agreement requires employer superannuation contributions of 15.4%, increasing annual staffing cost to approximately $299,000 (DEWR 2024). Over three years this is approximately $896,000. A further $50,000 one-off allowance for dashboard development and publication produces an indicative three-year cost of $946,000, rounded to $0.95 million. No major data-acquisition budget is required because the underlying datasets already exist.

### Option 2 — Targeted AI Entry Hiring Credit

**Mechanism.** If an occupation remains on the Watchlist for two consecutive quarters, employers creating an additional entry-level position could receive $250 per week for 26 weeks, up to $6,500 per worker. Eligibility would require a net increase in employer headcount, at least 20 hours of work per week and no replacement of an existing employee.

**Target.** Up to 10,000 new entrants annually in activated occupations.

**Cost.** Maximum direct expenditure would be $65 million annually. A 5% allowance for administration, compliance and auditing adds approximately $3.25 million, producing an indicative annual ceiling of $68.25 million. Workforce Australia wage subsidies currently provide up to $10,000 for eligible new ongoing positions (DEWR 2026b). JobMaker similarly required additional headcount and paid $200 per week for eligible workers aged 16–29 (Treasury 2020).

### Option 3 — AI-Augmented Entry Pathways Pilot

**Mechanism.** DEWR would co-fund employers to redesign junior roles around AI augmentation rather than preserving tasks that technology can perform efficiently. Employers would receive up to $8,000 for a 12-month placement, conditional on a development plan identifying tasks being automated, higher-value duties transferred to the employee, structured AI training and workplace supervision. Payments would be staged against continued employment.

**Target.** A two-year pilot of 5,000 workers in Watchlist occupations.

**Cost.** Direct employer payments would total a maximum $40 million. Allowing 10% for program administration and monitoring adds $4 million, while reserving $1 million for an independent evaluation produces a total two-year ceiling of $45 million. The $8,000 employer payment remains below the existing maximum $10,000 Workforce Australia wage subsidy (DEWR 2026b).

## Evaluation of Policy Options

Option 1 provides the strongest efficiency and feasibility because it extends DEWR's existing framework using existing government datasets. Its approximately $0.95 million three-year cost is low, although monitoring alone cannot preserve entry-level positions. Option 2 provides the most direct employment incentive by reducing the cost of hiring inexperienced workers, but carries the greatest deadweight risk because some subsidised employees would have been hired anyway. At up to $68.25 million annually, poor targeting could become expensive. Option 3 is more administratively demanding but addresses the structural problem by redesigning junior jobs around AI augmentation and human-capital development. At $45 million over two years, it is fiscally contained relative to a recurring hiring credit. **Options 1 and 3 therefore provide the strongest combination of efficiency, equity and long-term adaptation, while Option 2 should remain a contingency if stronger evidence of entry-level displacement emerges.**

### Options-by-Criteria Matrix

| Policy option | Efficiency | Equity | Feasibility | Fiscal cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. AI Entry-Level Employment Monitor** | High | Moderate | High | Low — approximately **$0.95 million over 3 years** |
| **2. Targeted AI Entry Hiring Credit** | Moderate | High | High–Moderate | High — up to approximately **$68.25 million per year** |
| **3. AI-Augmented Entry Pathways Pilot** | High | High | Moderate | Moderate — approximately **$45 million over 2 years** |

## Recommendation and Implementation

DEWR should implement Option 1 immediately and launch Option 3 as a limited pilot, while designing Option 2 for conditional activation. This reflects the evidence: DEWR identifies a tentative negative relationship between AI exposure and employment growth, while JSA finds no clear entry-level decline attributable to generative AI (DEWR 2026a; JSA 2025a).

From Q1 2027, the Office of the Chief Economist should publish entry-level indicators alongside DEWR's existing monitoring framework. JSA should maintain the underlying occupational exposure measures. By Q3 2027, DEWR should commence the 5,000-place AI-Augmented Entry Pathways pilot and commission its independent evaluation.

**KPIs** should include:

- 12-month employment retention;
- six-month post-subsidy retention;
- completion of structured AI training;
- employer take-up; and
- entry-level employment growth in participating occupations relative to comparable exposed occupations.

The hiring credit should activate only where DEWR confirms two consecutive quarters of deterioration.

The objective is not to prevent productive AI adoption. It is to ensure that automation of routine junior tasks does not remove the pathways through which inexperienced workers acquire the skills required for higher-value, AI-augmented work.
